# Commentary: The Regulatory Gap Between AI Transparency and Consumer Protection in Personalized Food Information

---

## Proposed Titles

1. **When Transparency Is Not Enough: The Regulatory Clash Between the EU AI Act and Food Information Law in Personalized Nutrition**

2. **Personalized Persuasion or Informed Choice? Bridging the Gap Between AI Regulation and Consumer Protection in Food Retail**

3. **Beyond Disclosure: Why the EU AI Act Fails to Address Manipulation in AI-Driven Food Recommendations**

---

## Abstract

The proliferation of artificial intelligence in food retail promises unprecedented personalization of nutritional guidance, yet exposes a critical regulatory lacuna between AI governance and consumer food protection. This commentary examines the tension between the European Union's AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689), which emphasizes transparency and risk classification, and the Food Information to Consumers Regulation (EU 1169/2011), designed to prevent misleading information in static labelling contexts. We argue that current regulatory frameworks, conceived for traditional commercial practices, are inadequate to address the persuasive dynamics of AI-driven food dialogues. While the AI Act prohibits manipulative subliminal techniques causing "significant harm," it does not capture the gradual erosion of consumer autonomy through hyper-personalized nudging that technically complies with transparency requirements. Drawing on recent empirical evidence demonstrating that algorithmic personalization can materially distort consumer behaviour, we propose that AI-generated food recommendations may constitute unfair commercial practices even when properly disclosed. We conclude with a framework for ethical AI deployment in food retail and call for sector-specific regulatory guidance that bridges the gap between AI transparency obligations and substantive consumer protection in nutritional decision-making.

**Keywords:** EU AI Act; Food Information Regulation; personalized nutrition; digital nudging; consumer autonomy; unfair commercial practices; algorithmic manipulation

## 1. Introduction

The intersection of artificial intelligence and food retail represents one of the most consequential yet underexamined domains of contemporary consumer protection. The global AI in retail market, valued at USD 14.03 billion in 2025, is projected to reach USD 138.3 billion by 2035, with personalized recommendations generating approximately 35% of online revenue (Grand View Research, 2025). In the grocery sector specifically, AI-powered shopping assistants and personalized nutrition tools have moved from experimental novelty to mainstream adoption, with 92% of retailers offering AI tools during the 2024 holiday season and chatbots handling up to 95% of customer interactions (Capital One Shopping, 2025).

This technological transformation occurs against a regulatory backdrop designed for an earlier era. The Food Information to Consumers Regulation (FIC; EU 1169/2011) establishes comprehensive requirements for food labelling, predicated on the assumption that information is static, standardized, and passively received by consumers exercising autonomous choice. The regulation explicitly prohibits information that is "misleading" regarding the "characteristics of the food" or that could "mislead the consumer by suggesting that the food possesses special characteristics when in fact all similar foods possess such characteristics" (Article 7). Yet these provisions were drafted for printed labels and standardized claims—not for dynamic, conversational AI systems capable of tailoring persuasive messages to individual psychological profiles in real-time.

The European Union's response to AI governance, the AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689), entered into force on 1 August 2024 and introduces a risk-based classification system with transparency obligations. Article 50 requires that AI systems designed to interact with natural persons must be designed such that individuals are "informed that they are interacting with an AI system." However, this transparency requirement operates on the assumption that disclosure neutralizes manipulation—a premise increasingly challenged by empirical research demonstrating that awareness of algorithmic influence does not immunize consumers against its effects (Frontiers in AI, 2025).

This commentary argues that the current regulatory architecture creates a dangerous gap: AI systems can be fully transparent about their artificial nature while simultaneously deploying sophisticated behavioural techniques that materially distort consumer food choices. The question is not merely whether consumers know they are speaking to an AI, but whether that AI is structurally designed to serve their nutritional autonomy or to maximize commercial outcomes through persuasive personalization.

## 2. The Regulatory Landscape: Parallel Frameworks, Divergent Logics

### 2.1 The EU AI Act: Risk Classification and Transparency

The AI Act represents the world's first comprehensive horizontal regulation of artificial intelligence, organizing AI systems into risk categories: prohibited practices, high-risk systems, limited-risk systems, and minimal-risk systems (European Commission, 2024). The regulation's application to the food sector has received surprisingly limited scholarly attention, despite the breadth of AI deployment across the agrifood value chain.

For AI systems providing personalized food information in retail environments, the critical question is classification. Article 6 defines high-risk systems by reference to harmonized Union legislation (Annex I) or specific use cases (Annex III). Food information systems do not appear explicitly in either annex, potentially relegating most food retail AI to the "limited risk" category subject primarily to transparency obligations under Article 50.

This classification has significant implications. While high-risk systems must implement risk management protocols (Article 9), data governance procedures (Article 10), technical documentation (Article 11), and human oversight mechanisms (Article 14), limited-risk systems face only disclosure requirements. A food retail chatbot providing personalized nutritional recommendations may thus operate with minimal regulatory oversight beyond ensuring consumers know they are interacting with AI—a far cry from the comprehensive consumer protections embedded in food-specific legislation.

Article 5 does establish absolute prohibitions, including AI systems deploying "subliminal techniques beyond a person's consciousness" or "purposefully manipulative or deceptive techniques" that materially distort behaviour causing "significant harm." The European Commission's February 2025 guidelines clarify that "material distortion of behaviour" should be interpreted consistently with the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (2005/29/EC). However, the "significant harm" threshold creates substantial interpretive ambiguity. Is the purchase of a less healthy food product due to AI-mediated persuasion "significant harm"? The cumulative health effects of systematically nudged dietary patterns may be substantial, yet individual purchasing decisions rarely meet the threshold of immediacy and severity implied by the prohibition.

### 2.2 Food Information Regulation: Static Labels in a Dynamic World

The FIC Regulation establishes that food information "shall not be misleading" regarding the food's characteristics, effects, or properties (Article 7). Information must be "accurate, clear and easy to understand for the consumer." These provisions extend beyond packaging to "any information concerning a food and made available to the final consumer by means of any medium" (Article 2), suggesting prima facie applicability to AI-generated food communications.

The Regulation on Nutrition and Health Claims (EC 1924/2006) further restricts statements linking foods to health benefits, requiring pre-authorization through scientific assessment by the European Food Safety Authority. Health claims must appear on an approved list (Commission Regulation 432/2012), with specific conditions of use. An AI system generating personalized health-related food recommendations faces a complex compliance landscape: generic nutritional information may be permissible, but product-specific health claims must adhere to authorized wording and conditions.

The fundamental tension emerges clearly: the FIC regime assumes a distinction between permitted "information" and prohibited "misleading claims," with the latter assessed against objective falsity or consumer deception. But AI-driven personalization operates in a different register. The information may be technically accurate—"this product contains calcium, which contributes to normal bone maintenance" (an authorized claim)—while the selection, timing, framing, and contextualization of that information is optimized to influence purchasing behaviour in ways that may or may not align with the consumer's autonomous preferences.

### 2.3 The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive: The Missing Link

The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (UCPD; 2005/29/EC) provides the horizontal framework for commercial fairness, prohibiting practices that are "contrary to the requirements of professional diligence" and "materially distort the economic behaviour" of average consumers. The Directive's 2024 amendments through Directive 2024/825 strengthen protections against greenwashing but do not directly address AI-mediated personalization.

The European Commission's October 2024 Digital Fairness Fitness Check found existing consumer protection laws "not sufficiently effective in tackling current and emerging consumer harms in the digital environment," estimating harmful online commercial practices cost EU consumers €7.9 billion annually (European Commission, 2024). The forthcoming Digital Fairness Act, expected mid-2026, will specifically address dark patterns, addictive design, and unfair personalization practices—but until then, a regulatory gap persists.

Critically, the UCPD applies to practices that "impair the consumer's ability to make an informed decision" (Article 2). AI-driven personalization, by design, shapes the information environment to influence decisions. The legal question becomes whether personalization optimized for commercial outcomes—rather than consumer welfare—constitutes an unfair practice even when each individual recommendation is technically accurate and the AI nature is disclosed.

## 3. The Paradox of Personalization: From Nudging to Manipulation

### 3.1 The Spectrum of Influence

Behavioural economics distinguishes nudging—"any aspect of the choice architecture that alters people's behaviour in a predictable way without forbidding any options or significantly changing their economic incentives" (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008)—from manipulation, which undermines autonomous decision-making through exploitation of psychological vulnerabilities. Recent scholarship on digital nudging in food retail has developed typologies identifying three mechanisms that "unduly intrude upon autonomy: (1) the effort to opt out; (2) affective influence, such as social reference messaging and emotional appeals; and (3) non-transparency, including of the nudge itself and of non-nudged alternative options" (Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition, 2025).

AI-driven personalization complicates this taxonomy. A 2025 systematic review in AIS Transactions on Human-Computer Interaction examining 74 papers on digital nudging in online supermarkets found widespread deployment of techniques including food swap suggestions, shopping cart calorie counters, and personalized health scores. While some nudges promote healthier choices consistent with consumer welfare, the commercial context creates persistent tension: "ethical considerations are paramount" and designers "must strike a balance between influence and user autonomy and stay away from manipulative techniques" (Advances in Consumer Research, 2025).

The concern intensifies with AI personalization. A 2025 Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence opinion piece argues that e-commerce platforms have transitioned from buying sites to "designers of situations designed to encourage impulsivity," with nudging becoming "institutionalized." The authors conclude that "persuasive design and AI-mediated personalization design are leading consumers to often forfeit their autonomy"—not through deception, but through sophisticated understanding of behavioural triggers deployed at scale.

### 3.2 Transparency as Insufficient Safeguard

The AI Act's reliance on transparency as the primary consumer protection for non-high-risk systems reflects an informational model of consumer empowerment: if consumers know they are interacting with AI, they can appropriately discount or contextualize its recommendations. This assumption is empirically questionable.

Research on algorithmic literacy demonstrates persistent gaps between awareness and protective behaviour. The 2024 Moffatt v. Air Canada decision, where a Canadian tribunal held the airline liable for its chatbot's misinformation despite the artificial nature being evident, illustrates the legal consequences of assuming disclosure obviates responsibility. More fundamentally, awareness of AI interaction does not neutralize the persuasive effects of personalization—particularly when those effects operate through accurate information strategically selected and framed.

A 2025 perspective in Frontiers in Aging on the ethics of AI in nutrition and behaviour change notes that "despite the publication of numerous AI ethical frameworks, guidelines and regulations, none have specifically focused on nutrition and behaviour change" (Abrahams & Raimundo, 2025). The authors propose a seven-pillar framework addressing fairness, transparency, privacy, accountability, safety, human oversight, and societal benefit—recognizing that disclosure alone cannot ensure ethical AI deployment.

### 3.3 The Consumer Protection Gap

The gap between AI transparency and substantive consumer protection is particularly acute in food retail for several reasons:

**Asymmetric expertise**: AI systems trained on vast behavioural datasets possess knowledge of persuasion techniques far exceeding consumer awareness. A 2025 review in Molecular Nutrition & Food Research identifies that "one of the most significant barriers to adoption is the lack of transparency in how AI systems generate dietary advice" and that "public trust is strongly tied to regulatory clarity and AI literacy" (Mundt, 2025).

**Health implications**: Unlike general retail, food choices have cumulative health consequences. Systematic nudging toward less healthy options—even through technically accurate information—can contribute to diet-related disease. The FIC's consumer protection objectives extend beyond transactional fairness to public health.

**Vulnerable populations**: The AI Act's Article 5(1)(b) prohibits exploitation of vulnerabilities due to age, disability, or social/economic situation. Children, elderly individuals, and those with diet-related health conditions may be disproportionately susceptible to personalized food recommendations, yet these groups are precisely those targeted by health-focused personalization.

**Cumulative effects**: Individual food purchasing decisions may not constitute "significant harm" under Article 5, but patterns of AI-influenced dietary behaviour over time can substantially affect health outcomes. The regulatory framework's focus on discrete interactions fails to capture cumulative harm.

## 4. Toward an Ethical Framework for AI in Food Retail

### 4.1 Beyond Compliance: Substantive Consumer Protection

We propose that regulatory compliance should be understood as a floor, not a ceiling, for AI deployment in food retail. Drawing on the analysis above and emerging best practices, we articulate five principles for ethical AI food recommendation systems:

**Principle 1: Nutritional Primacy**
AI systems providing food recommendations should be designed with consumer nutritional welfare as the primary optimization target, not commercial conversion. Where commercial and health objectives conflict, the system should default to nutritional benefit. This requires structural separation between recommendation algorithms and commercial incentive structures.

**Principle 2: Authorized Claims Compliance**
Product-specific health information must strictly comply with the Regulation on Nutrition and Health Claims. AI systems should be trained on authorized claim databases (e.g., EU Register of Nutrition and Health Claims) and prevented from generating implied health associations that circumvent the authorization regime through conversational framing.

**Principle 3: Transparent Personalization Logic**
Beyond disclosing AI interaction, systems should explain the basis for personalization: what data informs recommendations, what objectives the algorithm optimizes, and how consumers can modify personalization parameters. This operationalizes the AI Act's transparency requirement substantively rather than formally.

**Principle 4: Autonomy-Preserving Design**
Following emerging scholarship on digital nudging, AI food systems should minimize opt-out effort, avoid affective manipulation techniques (false urgency, social pressure, emotional appeals), and ensure non-personalized alternatives remain visible and accessible. Personalization should enhance informed choice, not substitute for it.

**Principle 5: Vulnerable Population Safeguards**
Systems should implement heightened protections for users identifiable as potentially vulnerable—including age-appropriate interfaces, medical condition sensitivity, and economic circumstance awareness. Where vulnerability cannot be assessed, conservative defaults should apply.

### 4.2 Operationalizing the Framework

For food retailers and technology providers, operationalizing these principles requires:

1. **Risk assessment**: Systematic evaluation of AI food information systems against both AI Act classification criteria and food-specific regulatory requirements, recognizing that systems may trigger high-risk obligations if health data is processed or vulnerable populations targeted.

2. **Data governance**: Clear protocols for training data, ensuring models incorporate authorized health claims, current nutritional guidelines (e.g., EFSA dietary reference values), and avoid reinforcing dietary biases present in historical consumption data.

3. **Human oversight**: Mechanisms for human review of AI-generated recommendations, particularly where health claims are implicated, consistent with the AI Act's Article 14 requirements for high-risk systems and as best practice for limited-risk systems.

4. **Post-market monitoring**: Ongoing assessment of recommendation patterns for systematic bias toward commercial over nutritional objectives, with correction mechanisms when divergence is identified.

5. **Incident reporting**: Protocols for identifying and reporting instances where AI recommendations may have contributed to consumer harm, consistent with AI Act Article 73 obligations.

## 5. Conclusion and Future Outlook

The deployment of AI in food retail exemplifies a broader regulatory challenge: horizontal AI governance frameworks designed for general application may inadequately address sector-specific consumer protection concerns. The EU AI Act's transparency-focused approach to limited-risk systems, while appropriate for many contexts, provides insufficient protection where AI-mediated persuasion intersects with health-related consumer decisions.

The FIC Regulation's prohibition on misleading information and the UCPD's prohibition on practices that materially distort consumer behaviour provide doctrinal foundations for addressing manipulative AI food recommendations. However, these frameworks require interpretive development to capture the novel harms of personalized persuasion that operates through accurate information strategically deployed.

We call on policymakers to:

1. **Issue sector-specific guidance** on AI Act application to food retail, clarifying risk classification for health-adjacent AI systems and establishing expectations for transparency beyond mere AI disclosure.

2. **Update FIC Regulation interpretation** to address AI-generated food information explicitly, ensuring that personalized recommendations face equivalent scrutiny to product labelling claims.

3. **Expedite Digital Fairness Act adoption** with provisions specifically addressing AI-driven personalization in contexts with health implications.

4. **Establish industry codes of practice** for AI food recommendation systems, developed through multi-stakeholder processes including consumer organizations, public health authorities, and technology providers.

The promise of AI in nutrition—personalized guidance helping consumers navigate complex dietary decisions—is substantial. Realizing that promise requires regulatory frameworks ensuring AI serves consumer autonomy rather than exploiting behavioural vulnerabilities. Transparency about AI interaction is necessary but insufficient; substantive consumer protection demands that personalization be designed for nutritional benefit, constrained by food law, and accountable to the consumers it purports to serve.
